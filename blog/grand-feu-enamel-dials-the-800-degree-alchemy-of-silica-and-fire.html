1. The Immortality of Vitreous Enamel in Watchmaking

Printed lacquered dials, painted metal discs, and galvanic coatings all share a common vulnerability: they age. Over decades, ultraviolet sunlight fades pigments, environmental humidity causes brass substrates to corrode, and synthetic varnishes turn yellow or develop hairline crazing.

**Grand Feu Enamel** (literally "Great Fire" in French) is the sole dial craft that defies time. An enamel dial created in the 17th century remains as pristine, luminous, and vivid today as the afternoon it emerged from the watchmaker kiln oven.

Enamel is not paint; it is soft silica glass fused permanently onto solid precious metal under intense heat. At AuraDialVale, our enamel atelier practices this perilous historical alchemy, accepting high scrap rates to produce dials of unmatched depth and timeless porcelain beauty.

2. The Chemical Composition of Enamel: Silica, Flux, and Metal Oxides

Raw vitreous enamel is composed of pure silica quartz crystals (\(\text{SiO}_2\)) mixed with alkaline fluxes (potash and soda) to lower the melting point, and borax to provide elasticity.

Chromatic colors are achieved by adding precise metal oxides:

  • Cobalt Oxide: Yields deep royal sapphire and celestial midnight blues.
  • Copper Oxide: Produces vibrant turquoise, emerald green, or flame red.
  • Gold Chloride / Purple of Cassius: Generates radiant ruby reds and amethyst purples.
  • Tin / Antimony Oxide: Creates opaque, milky alabaster porcelain whites.

3. Preparing the Raw Enamel: Mortar Grinding and Water Washing

Raw enamel arrives at our atelier as hard, translucent glass pebbles called *frit*.

The enameller grinds the frit manually in an agate mortar and pestle under distilled water. The grinding must achieve exact particle consistencyâ€”if the grains are too coarse, the fired surface will be grainy; if too fine, microscopic air bubbles will become trapped during fusion, creating pinhole defects. The pulverized powder is washed repeatedly in purified water to remove all dust and impurities.

4. The Substrate: Solid 18k Gold and Counter-Enamelling Protocols

Enamel cannot be fused onto common metals because differing thermal expansion coefficients will shatter the glass upon cooling.

We craft all enamel dial plates from **Solid 18k Yellow or White Gold**. Crucially, the reverse side of the dial must undergo **Counter-Enamelling** (*contre-Ã©mail*). By applying an identical layer of enamel to the back of the gold plate, mechanical tension is balanced equally on both sides during cooling, preventing the dial from warping like a dried leaf.

5. The 800Â°C Crucible: The Multi-Stage Firing Ritual

A single Grand Feu dial undergoes between **6 and 10 separate kiln firings**:

1. The artisan dusts a microscopic layer of moist enamel powder onto the gold blank using a fine badger-hair quill brush.
2. The dial is placed on a refractory ceramic disc and inserted into an electric kiln heated to exactly **800Â°C to 840Â°C** for 45 to 60 seconds.
3. Through the kiln peephole, the artisan watches as the dry powder suddenly liquefies, flowing into a glassy, glowing orange lake of molten silica.
4. The dial is swiftly withdrawn and cooled on a preheated steel anvil, carefully planning the surface flat between firings.

7. Fired Enamel Typography: Hand-Painting Indices with Oxide Pastes

The Roman numerals, railroad minute tracks, and brand inscriptions on our Grand Feu dials are not printed with modern inks. They are hand-painted using black or cobalt metal oxide paste mixed with lavender essential oil, followed by an additional final kiln firing at 750Â°C that fuses the typography permanently into the glass substrate.

8. The Heartbreak Rate: Managing Thermal Shock, Bubbles, and Micro-Cracks

Grand Feu enamelling is an art of immense heartbreak. At any point during the 7th or 8th firing, a microscopic thermal shock can cause the dial to crack with a faint, tragic "tink" sound, rendering days of work completely useless. In our atelier, **between 40% and 50% of all dials fail inspection** and must be discarded.

In our bespoke astronomical series, master artisans execute **Miniature Enamel Painting**. Using single-bristle marten-hair brushes, the artist paints celestial star maps, lunar surfaces, and zodiac constellations directly onto fired white enamel using oil-suspended metal pigments.

Each color layer requires an individual firing in the kiln. The artisan must know the precise thermal sensitivity of each oxide: reds fire at 700Â°C, blues at 780Â°C, and whites at 820Â°C. One miscalculated firing order will burn and blacken delicate pastel shades.

11. Lapping and Flat Diamond Polishing (Le Pierrage)

After the final 800Â°C firing, the dial surface is wavy and slightly domed due to surface tension. The dial undergoes **Pierrage** (stone lapping):

The artisan rubs the glass surface against fine carborundum stones and Arkansas oilstones under running water, grinding down high spots until the enamel is flat within 2 microns. A final flash-firing in the kiln melts the surface microscopic scratches, restoring high-gloss vitreous clarity.
